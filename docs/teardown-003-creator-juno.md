# Teardown 003 — Creator: Juno (Douyin)

Checked 2026-10-29. Method: full enumeration of the profile's work list (38 works, the page reported no more), a deep sample of 10 works (3 highest-liked, 2 earliest, 3 newest, 2 mid-tier), and the visible top comments on the three biggest works. Browser playback was muted by the platform, so narration voice and music could not be verified and are marked as such below. Numbers are as displayed on the day of the check.

## Identity and scale
- Account name Juno; bio, in translation: "Whenever I meet an interesting topic, I want to make it into a small film for you. Mathematics | History | Psychology | Trivia."
- 20k followers; 728k total likes; 38 works, all published inside one month (2026-09-30 to 2026-10-28) — better than one per day. No profile collections; nearly every work carries the Vibe Knowledge campaign tag.
- Concentration is extreme: the three pinned works hold about 529k of the 728k total likes (about 73%).

## The catalogue
Named classic problems, retold as stories: the secretary problem (the 37% rule), Dunbar's number, the German tank problem, Benford's law, the prisoner's dilemma tournament, the spotlight effect, the wisdom of crowds, random walks, six degrees of separation, sunk cost, phantom traffic jams. Later works add history (the Twelve Bronze Heads, the money timeline) and timed hotspots (an LPL Monte Carlo post-mortem, a Tesla Roadster explainer, a Nobel contract-theory episode).

## Format specification (observed across the sample)
- Vertical, about 2 minutes (sampled range 1:37-2:45). Two-dimensional illustrated animation throughout; no live footage; most works carry the platform's "AI-generated content" declaration.
- Every episode has a film-style title in book brackets, published under a question headline ("How many of the Twelve Bronze Heads came home?").
- The opening seconds show a concrete prop or scene (numbered cards on a clothesline for the dating problem; an incoming-call screen; a poker hand with an "expectation" gauge), a large-text statement of the situation, often with a smaller English sub-line.
- Structure: a story or case hook, then a named study, model or historical event, then a portable payoff — one number or rule (37%; 150 people; production totals from serial numbers alone) — then a closing question or emotional turn.
- Subtitles are the information carrier: large white text, keywords and numbers highlighted in yellow, orange or red, dense enough to follow the whole film muted.

## Production process (evidence levels kept separate)
- Direct evidence: a uniform illustration style, AI-generation declarations on most works, a solo account, and a daily-plus cadence over a full month.
- Reasonable inference: topics are picked from a library of classic problems, written to the fixed story-to-rule script shape, and produced through one fixed illustration-and-voice pipeline. Some later works are the author's own data modelling (the LPL simulation carries a "personal opinion" declaration instead of the AI one).
- Not evidenced: no comment or work names any tool; viewers ask how the videos are made and receive no public answer. Voice (human vs TTS) and music identity are unverified — playback was muted.

## Why it worked
1. **The topics are pre-sold.** Every episode is a named problem with a counter-intuitive answer and a portable number. The name itself is the credibility; no episode has to argue it is worth watching.
2. **Emotion at the door, reason as the payoff.** Headlines open on universal anxieties (which person is "the one", who could you call at 3 a.m., why a ringing phone tightens your chest) and close with mathematics. The top work's comments argue seriously about whether 37% applies to both sides; the phone episode's 13k comments are mostly self-recognition.
3. **Save and share ratios are high.** On the top works, saves run about 19-27% of likes and shares about 23-26% (one phone-episode share figure displayed higher than its like count and is recorded as displayed). A usable rule is content people keep and forward.
4. **Cadence times campaign leverage.** Thirty-eight works in a month, all inside the official campaign's traffic-support window, with the three hits pinned as the profile's storefront.

## The counterexample inside the same catalogue
The newest week breaks the pattern: the LPL post-mortem sits at 15 likes, the money history at 40, the Einstein-brain episode at 90 — while the next day's Bronze Heads episode reached about 1,299 within a day. Same format, same account: the drop tracks topic choice, not production. Off-formula niche hotspots collapse; the format dividend decays and the topic formula is the actual asset.

## What this account adopts from it
- A film-style episode title for every episode, alongside the publishing headline (adopted into our packaging from this teardown).
- End on one portable rule or number — the same shape Ep1 already uses (perceived speed = distance / time); this catalogue is 38 works of validation for it.
- Build the topic library from named effects, laws and classic problems first — now codified in `docs/topic-selection-standard.md`.
- Frequency is not a substitute for formula fit; our lower-cadence, higher-gate model makes topic discipline more important, not less.
